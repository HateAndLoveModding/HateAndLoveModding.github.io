One of the parts of refining the RAG is to improve the chunking. When you have massive amounts of data, you need to split the data into smaller chunks. One of the desicions is exactly how small those chunks are. Bigger chunk sizes mean the data is less distinguishable. The chunks gets converted to a high dimensional vector of numbers. Every chunk gets converted to a vector of the same number of dimensions. This means that a smaller chunk allows the vectors to be more unique and ensures that relevant chunks will be returned. The problem is that often you don't just that piece of information. You want something closely related or information around that. For example if you have a RAG with song lyrics and you want to know the lyrics to a specific part of the song but you don't remember the song name or title but you do not know some of the lyrics in the chorus but you want to know the words in the second half of the song. You could have each line be it's own chunk, but that would not be helpful because it would just return the line of the song that you already know. One thing you should do with a RAG is also store information of where that data came from in each chunk. In this case that would mean storing the song name and artist and line number as well as the actual line. When the RAG returns the line that you already know, you can figure out the song name and artist but that only helps you a little bit. You can address this issue by having the chunks be larger. This makes the chunks less distinguishable so you may not always get the right chunk if you can't remember enough of the lyrics but if it does return the right chunk there is a better chance that the line that you want to figure out is also in that chunk and the LLM can figure it out. Even if the line is not in that chunk, you may be able to use the lyrics or information in that chunk to do another retrieval and get the chunk that the line is actually in. So it is definitely a balancing act. This is why you do mean reciprocal rank (MRR) testing. In MRR testing you determine which chunk is the most relevant and then you write a test case and perform retrieval on it. The chunk that you determined is most relevant should be the first chunk returned. If it is returned in a later chunk that is kinda okay but puts more work on the LLM to parse the data. If it is not returned at all, the LLM might hallucinate or it just won't be able to answer the question. I did not know about MRR until the very end of field session and I did not have enough time to do much MRR testing though I did have Claude generate some test cases and it seemed to perform pretty good. I do not have any data about specifically how well the RAG performed with the MRR testing. If the RAG is not performing well on the MRR testing, it may be beneficial to reduce the chunk size. If the MRR testing is good but the LLM is still struggling to answer the question, then you may have a problem where your chunk size is too small and you are not giving enough context to the LLM. I tried to balance the chunk size as best I could. Generally I would try to fit the information from one table into one chunk unless the chunk was bigger than a certain threshold. One of the other things that I found to be very helpful is to convert the plain text to markdown. I leared that LLMs work very well with markdown because it gives better structure. It essentially organizses the data better for the LLM and is very easy to add information about where the data is found. For example you could have the first header be the artist and then the second header be the song. When you are ingesting the data, you add that data to the chunk. You could even add a genre header if that would be helpful. I mentioned MRR testing but I didn't do that until very late. I started out by just looking at the similarity scores of the input vs the output. As I implemented HyDE and experimented with chunk sizes and changed from plain text to markdown, the retrieval relevance scores increased from 0.74 to 0.86.
